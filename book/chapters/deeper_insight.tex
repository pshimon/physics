\chapter{Deeper Insight}
\label{cm:di}

Classical mechanics is usually presented as the most settled part of
physics: three laws, a variational principle, and a semester of
worked examples. By the time a student reaches quantum theory or
general relativity, mechanics has already receded into the
background as a body of technique rather than a live subject. This
impression is not wrong, exactly, but it is incomplete. If one
presses on a few of the concepts that the standard course treats as
self-evident --- \emph{force}, \emph{constraint}, \emph{system} ---
the apparatus turns out to rest on assumptions that are easy to
state and easy to violate. What happens is instructive: relaxing a
single tacit assumption does not merely complicate the bookkeeping,
it can change the physics outright, produce equations that look
paradoxical on first encounter, or reveal that two "obviously
equivalent" formulations of mechanics are not equivalent at all.

\section{Configuration Space}
\label{cm:cs}

Let's discuss configuration space in some details. For $N$ point particles it
is 
\[\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^3 \times \dots
\times\mathbb{R}^3}_{N \text{times}}\] 
which naturally isomorphic to
$\mathbb{R}^{3 N}$as vector space, topological space, and smooth manifold. But
they emphasize different structures and interpretations. For example natural
rotation is $SO(3) \subset SO(3N)$ not $SO(3N)$, kinetic energy has natural block
structure. For identical particles one should  identify configurations related by 
a permutation: physical configuration space is more properly
\[Q_{\rm{identical}}=(\mathbb{R}^3)^N/S_N.\]
But the most important subtlety is
that distance in configuration space may have no physical sense. 

Two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator and two
identical, spatially distant, non-interacting one-dimensional oscillators have
exactly the same configuration space, $\mathbb{R}^2$, and, once the two
one-dimensional coordinates are laid side by side, exactly the same
Hamiltonian. This coincidence might seem to threaten the idea that
configuration space carries direct physical meaning: if the bare pair $(Q,H)$
cannot distinguish ``one particle moving in a plane'' from ``two particles
moving on separate, distant lines,'' in what sense does configuration space
represent physical positions at all? We examine this coincidence carefully,
show that it persists at every level of structure computable from $(Q,H)$ alone
--- classical trajectories, the symplectic structure, the quantum spectrum and
its degeneracies --- and then identify precisely what additional data is needed
to tell the two systems apart: (i) an embedding of $Q$ into physical space
together with the action of the spatial isometry group, which is geometrically
realized in one case and merely accidental in the other; (ii) statistics and
particle-content data, which matter only when exchange effects are actually
observable; and (iii) the algebra of interactions that locality in physical
space picks out as natural, which governs how each system couples to a generic
environment. We package this as the \emph{physical dressing} of a bare
Hamiltonian system and argue that configuration space, taken on its own, is
best understood as a representation-dependent bookkeeping device rather than a
self-standing arena of physical positions: its physical reading requires this
further data as an independent postulate, not something read off the space
itself.

In elementary treatments of analytical mechanics, configuration space is often
introduced as though it simply \emph{were} the space of physical positions of a
system: a point $q\in Q$ is ``where the system is,'' and the Hamiltonian
$H:T^*Q\to\mathbb{R}$ tells us how that point moves. This picture is useful
and, for the simplest systems, harmless. But it invites a stronger reading on
which the pair $(Q,H)$ is taken to \emph{fix} the physical system being
described --- as if knowing the configuration manifold and the Hamiltonian
function on it were enough to know what kind of physical situation one is
dealing with.

A simple example shows this stronger reading to be false. Consider, on the one
hand, a single particle of mass $m$ moving in a plane under an isotropic
harmonic restoring force (the two-dimensional isotropic oscillator), and, on
the other hand, two particles of equal mass $m$, held very far apart, each
independently oscillating along its own line under a harmonic force of the same
strength. Written in the natural coordinates for each case, the two systems
have configuration space $Q$ and Hamiltonian $H$
\emph{identically}. There is no sense in which the bare mathematical data
$(Q,H)$ --- or, equivalently, the classical trajectories it generates, the
associated Schr\"odinger equation, or the energy spectrum obtained by
quantizing it --- differs between the two cases. Yet no one would say that a
single particle vibrating in a plane and two distant, independent oscillators
are ``the same physical system.'' Something is being smuggled in from outside
$(Q,H)$ whenever we say what the system \emph{is}.

The purpose of this note is to make that ``something'' explicit. We first state
the coincidence precisely and trace its consequences through the classical and
quantum formalism, to establish that it really is exact and not an artifact of
sloppy bookkeeping. We then identify three independent kinds of structure ---
geometric embedding and symmetry realization, locality of coupling to an
environment, and particle statistics --- whose specification is what actually
distinguishes the two systems, and show why each one is invisible to $(Q,H)$
alone. We collect this into a general schema for what a \emph{physically
interpreted} Hamiltonian system requires beyond its bare dynamical data, and
close with related examples from classical and quantum physics, and a remark on
the philosophical literature on configuration space and on analogous
underdetermination arguments elsewhere in physics.

None of this impugns the ordinary practical use of configuration space, and
none of it is really in tension with anything in the standard formalism of
mechanics --- once one is precise about it, the resolution is straightforward.
But the example is a useful corrective against a casual habit, common in both
physics pedagogy and in some philosophical discussions of quantum theory, of
treating configuration space as though it were self-interpreting.

\paragraph{System A (the planar oscillator).} A single particle of mass $m$
moves in a plane, subject to an isotropic harmonic potential centered on a
fixed equilibrium point. Writing $(q_1,q_2)$ for Cartesian coordinates of its
position relative to equilibrium, the configuration space is $Q_A=\mathbb{R}^2$
and the Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
H_A(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)=\frac{p_1^2+p_2^2}{2m}
    +\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2\left(q_1^2+q_2^2\right).
\label{HA}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{System B (two distant oscillators).} Two particles, each of mass
$m$, sit at fixed, well-separated base points in physical space and each
oscillates, independently and without interaction, along its own fixed axis
under a harmonic restoring force of the same strength $\omega$. Writing
$q_1,q_2$ for the (signed, one-dimensional) displacements of particle~1 and
particle~2 from their respective equilibria, the configuration space is
$Q_B=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\cong\mathbb{R}^2$ and the Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
H_B(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)=\frac{p_1^2+p_2^2}{2m}
    +\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2\left(q_1^2+q_2^2\right).
\label{HB}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Bare dynamical equivalence]
The map $\iota:T^*Q_A\to T^*Q_B$ given by the identity on coordinates,
    $(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)\mapsto(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)$, is a symplectomorphism
    satisfying $H_B\circ\iota = H_A$. Consequently the two bare Hamiltonian
    systems $(Q_A,H_A)$ and $(Q_B,H_B)$ are indistinguishable by any invariant
    constructed from the Hamiltonian and the canonical symplectic structure
    alone.
\end{proposition}

The proof is immediate: \eqref{HA} and \eqref{HB} are literally the same
function of the same symbols, so the identity map trivially intertwines them
and preserves $dq_1\wedge dp_1+dq_2\wedge dp_2$. The content of the proposition
is not the (trivial) verification but the list of things it entails. Every
quantity computable purely from $(Q,H)$ agrees between the two systems:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Classical trajectories.} Hamilton's equations give
    \[q_i(t)=A_i\cos(\omega t+\varphi_i),\quad i=1,2,\]
        with the same relation
        between amplitudes, phases, and initial data in both cases.
\item \textbf{Conserved quantities.} $H$ itself, and, since
    \eqref{HA}--\eqref{HB} are isotropic in $(q_1,q_2)$, the quantity $L\equiv
        q_1p_2-q_2p_1$ Poisson-commutes with $H$ in both cases and generates
        the one-parameter family of canonical transformations
        \[R_\theta:(q_1,q_2)\mapsto(q_1\cos\theta-q_2\sin\theta,\quad
        q_1\sin\theta+q_2\cos\theta),\]
        together with the analogous action on
        momenta, under which $H$ is invariant in both cases.
\item \textbf{Quantization.} Canonical quantization $q_i\mapsto \hat q_i$,
    $p_i\mapsto-i\hbar\partial_{q_i}$ produces the identical partial
        differential operator, hence the identical time-dependent Schr\"odinger
        equation for a wavefunction $\psi(q_1,q_2,t)$, in both cases.
\item \textbf{Spectrum.} The energy eigenvalues are
    $E_{n_1n_2}=\hbar\omega(n_1+n_2+1)$, $n_1,n_2=0,1,2,\dots$, with the same
        $(n+1)$-fold degeneracy of the level $E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1)$, in both
        cases.
\item \textbf{Statistical mechanics.} The classical and quantum partition
    functions, and every thermodynamic quantity derived from them, agree.
\end{itemize}

In short, the coincidence is not superficial: it survives quantization,
survives the identification of conserved quantities and dynamical symmetries,
and survives the passage to statistical mechanics. Any account of what
distinguishes the two systems must therefore appeal to something that is not a
function of $(Q,H)$ alone.


We identify three independent sources of physical structure that are not
visible in $(Q,H)$, each of which distinguishes System A from System B once
made explicit.

\subsection{Embedding in physical space and the status of the rotational symmetry}

The transformation $R_\theta$ identified above is, as a transformation of the
abstract manifold $Q=\mathbb{R}^2$, a symmetry of $H$ in \emph{both} systems.
But this bare fact does not tell us whether $R_\theta$ corresponds to anything
happening in physical space. To ask that question we must specify an embedding
of $Q$ into physical space $\Sigma$ (taken here to be $\mathbb{R}^3$, or a
plane within it) that says what each coordinate \emph{is}.

For System A, the natural embedding sends the single particle's configuration
to its actual position,
\[
\iota_A(q_1,q_2)=\mathbf{X}^{(0)}+q_1\hat{e}_1+q_2\hat{e}_2 \ \in\ \Sigma,
\]
and $R_\theta$ is exactly the pushforward, under $\iota_A$, of a genuine
rotation of physical space about the fixed point $\mathbf{X}^{(0)}$: rotating
the lab by angle $\theta$ carries the particle from $\iota_A(q_1,q_2)$ to
$\iota_A(R_\theta(q_1,q_2))$. The symmetry is \emph{geometric}: it is
implemented by an isometry of $\Sigma$, it corresponds to a real conserved
angular momentum exchangeable with other systems via torque, and it is what
makes $L=q_1p_2-q_2p_1$ deserve the name ``angular momentum'' rather than being
merely a conserved combination of phase-space coordinates.

For System B, the natural embedding sends the pair of independent
one-dimensional configurations to the positions of two separate particles,
\[
\iota_B(q_1,q_2)=\Big(\mathbf{X}_1^{(0)}+q_1\hat{n}_1,\ \
\mathbf{X}_2^{(0)}+q_2\hat{n}_2\Big)\ \in\ \Sigma\times\Sigma,
\]
with $\mathbf{X}_1^{(0)}\neq\mathbf{X}_2^{(0)}$ (this is what ``distant''
means). Here $R_\theta$ cannot be the pushforward of \emph{any} isometry of
$\Sigma$.

\begin{proposition}[Non-transfer of the rotational symmetry]
For $\mathbf{X}_1^{(0)}\neq\mathbf{X}_2^{(0)}$, there is no isometry $\rho$ of
    $\Sigma$ such that $\iota_B\circ R_\theta=(\rho,\rho)\circ\iota_B$ holds
    for all $\theta$ (other than $\theta\in\pi\mathbb{Z}$).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An isometry $\rho$ of physical space acts \emph{pointwise}: the image
    $\rho(\mathbf{y})$ of a point $\mathbf{y}\in\Sigma$ depends only on
    $\mathbf{y}$ itself. In particular, the would-be image of particle 2's
    position, $\rho\big(\mathbf{X}_2^{(0)}+q_2\hat n_2\big)$, can depend only
    on $q_2$ --- it cannot depend on $q_1$, since particle~1's displacement
    plays no role in specifying the point $\mathbf{X}_2^{(0)}+q_2\hat{n}_2$
    that $\rho$ is asked to act on. But the second component of
    $\iota_B(R_\theta(q_1,q_2))$ is
    $\mathbf{X}_2^{(0)}+(q_1\sin\theta+q_2\cos\theta)\hat n_2$, which for
    $\sin\theta\neq 0$ depends non-trivially on $q_1$. No pointwise map can
    reproduce this dependence, so no single isometry $\rho$ can serve as
    $\iota_B\circ R_\theta=(\rho,\rho)\circ\iota_B$ for such $\theta$.
\end{proof}

The obstruction is not a matter of fine-tuning
$\mathbf{X}_1^{(0)},\mathbf{X}_2^{(0)},\hat n_1,\hat n_2$; it holds however
these are chosen, and it fails only in the degenerate limit
$\mathbf{X}_1^{(0)}=\mathbf{X}_2^{(0)}$ --- precisely the limit in which System
B stops being ``two distant oscillators'' and coincides with System A. So
$R_\theta$ is, for System B, a symmetry of the Hamiltonian with no geometric
referent: it mixes the coordinates of two causally and spatially independent
objects in a way that no rotation, translation, or reflection of the physical
world could ever do. It is what is sometimes called a \emph{hidden},
\emph{accidental}, or purely \emph{dynamical} symmetry --- real as a statement
about the equations of motion, but carrying none of the physical content
(conservation via Noether's theorem applied to an actual spacetime symmetry,
exchangeability of angular momentum with other systems, appearance in a
Wigner-Eckart-type selection rule tied to real rotations) that the same-looking
symmetry carries in System A.

\begin{remark}
This is not special to the isotropic case $\omega_1=\omega_2$. If the two
    frequencies differ, $H=\frac{p_1^2+p_2^2}{2m}+\frac12
    m(\omega_1^2q_1^2+\omega_2^2q_2^2)$ still describes, term for term, both
    ``one particle in an anisotropic planar potential'' and ``two distant,
    differently-tuned oscillators,'' and the argument of Section~2 goes through
    unchanged. The isotropic case is simply the sharpest illustration, because
    there the enhanced symmetry make the geometric/accidental distinction most
    conspicuous.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Locality and the coupling to a generic environment}
\label{loc}

A second, complementary way to separate the two systems is to ask not about
their isolated dynamics but about how they couple to the rest of the world.
Isolation is always an idealization; a real oscillator sits in some environment
(radiation field, phonon bath, measuring apparatus), and it is a basic fact
about physical interactions that they are \emph{local}: an environmental degree
of freedom that is spatially near one object couples appreciably to that object
and negligibly to a second object located far away.

For System B this has an immediate and specific consequence. Because particle~1
and particle~2 occupy distant regions of space, generic environmental
influences factorize: to good approximation, particle~1 couples to a bath $B_1$
associated with its own neighborhood and particle~2 couples to an independent
bath $B_2$ associated with its neighborhood,
\begin{equation}
H_{SE}\ \approx\ q_1\otimes \hat B_1 \ +\ q_2\otimes \hat B_2 ,
\label{localcoupling}
\end{equation}
with $\hat B_1,\hat B_2$ statistically independent (uncorrelated) environmental
operators. This is simply the statement that two distant systems have
independent noise sources --- an instance of what is often called cluster
decomposition. Standard decoherence arguments \cite{zurek2003} then predict
that a coupling of the form \eqref{localcoupling} drives the joint state
toward one that is (to a good approximation) diagonal in a basis built
separately out of $q_1$ and $q_2$ --- an emergent ``which-oscillator'' pointer
basis that tracks the pre-existing particle structure.

For System A, by contrast, there is only one particle, occupying one region of
space, and a generic local environmental influence acts at that single
location. Such an influence couples to some particular direction $\hat
n\cdot(q_1,q_2)$ in configuration space determined by the geometry of the
perturbation (e.g.\ the direction of a stray field at the particle's location),
not separately and independently to $q_1$ and $q_2$; arranging two independent
environments that couple exclusively along the $q_1$-axis and the $q_2$-axis
respectively, at one and the same spatial point, is not something locality
delivers for free, the way \eqref{localcoupling} is delivered for free in
System B --- it would need to be imposed by hand. There is consequently no
generic physical reason for a local environment to single out the particular
coordinate split $(q_1,q_2)$, as opposed to any rotated pair
$(q_1',q_2')=R_\theta(q_1,q_2)$, as the one relevant to decoherence.

\begin{remark}
This argument is offered as a physical heuristic, not a theorem: it says what
    is \emph{generic} given locality, not what is strictly forced by $(Q,H)$
    (which, after all, says nothing at all about any environment). That is
    exactly the point --- the distinction it draws on is additional structure,
    namely a specification of which couplings to the wider world are
    ``local''/``natural'' for the system in question. This is close in spirit
    to the observation, developed rigorously by Zanardi, Lidar, and Lloyd
    \cite{zll}, that a tensor-product (subsystem)
    decomposition of a quantum state space is not read off the Hilbert space or
    the Hamiltonian in isolation but is induced by the algebra of operationally
    accessible interactions and measurements. Here the analogous claim is made
    already at the level of which classical/environmental couplings are
    physically natural.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Statistics and the configuration space of identical particles}

A third consideration concerns particle content and exchange statistics, and it
is worth being precise about exactly when it does, and does not, resolve the
coincidence.

If the two oscillators of System B are quantum-mechanically identical particles
\emph{and} the physical situation is one in which exchange effects are actually
observable, then the classical configuration space appropriate to the pair is
not $\mathbb{R}^2$ but the quotient $\mathbb{R}^2/\mathbb{Z}_2$ obtained by
identifying $(q_1,q_2)\sim(q_2,q_1)$ --- a space that is genuinely different,
as a manifold (it has boundary, corresponding to the coincidence locus
$q_1=q_2$), from the configuration space $\mathbb{R}^2$ of System A. This is
the classical configuration-space counterpart of the requirement that the
quantum wavefunction be (anti)symmetrized, and it is the starting point of the
classic analysis of configuration spaces of identical particles by Leinaas and
Myrheim \cite{leinaas+myrheim}
On this route, the puzzle is dissolved already at the level
of $Q$: System A and a genuinely exchange-symmetric System B simply do not have
the same configuration space once the manifold is defined correctly, as the
space of \emph{physically distinct} configurations rather than of coordinate
pairs.

But this resolution requires exchange effects to be observable, and
``distant,'' taken seriously, is precisely the regime in which they are not: if
the two particles' wavefunctions never overlap, no observable is sensitive to
(anti)symmetrization, the coincidence locus $q_1=q_2$ is never explored, and
$\mathbb{R}^2/\mathbb{Z}_2$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$ are observationally
indistinguishable restricted to the relevant region of configuration space. In
that regime --- and equally, trivially, if the two oscillators are simply not
identical particles to begin with (different species, different traps) ---
statistics does no work, $Q_B\cong\mathbb{R}^2$ stands as given, and the
coincidence with System A is  sharp . So this route
resolves the puzzle only conditionally, and precisely when it does, what is
doing the resolving is not the bare Hamiltonian but a further fact: a decision
about particle identity together with a fact about whether the dynamics in
question ever probes the exchange-sensitive region of configuration space. That
further fact is, again, exactly the kind of data invisible to $(Q,H)$ alone.

\subsection{The Physical Dressing of a Hamiltonian System}

The three considerations above are independent of one another --- a system can
have the wrong embedding, the wrong locality structure, or the wrong statistics
without touching the other two --- but they share a common moral, which we now
state more systematically.

\begin{definition}[Bare Hamiltonian system]
A \emph{bare Hamiltonian system} is a triple $(Q,\omega_{\mathrm{can}},H)$: a
    configuration manifold $Q$, the canonical symplectic form on $T^*Q$, and a
    Hamiltonian function $H$. Two bare systems are \emph{dynamically
    equivalent} if related by a symplectomorphism intertwining their
    Hamiltonians.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Physical dressing]
A \emph{physical dressing} of a bare Hamiltonian system
    $(Q,\omega_{\mathrm{can}},H)$ is a tuple
    $\mathcal{D}=(\Sigma,G,\iota,\Pi,\mathcal{A})$, where: $\Sigma$ is an
    ambient physical space(time) with isometry (or, relativistically, symmetry)
    group $G$; $\iota:Q\to\Sigma^N$ is an embedding assigning to each
    configuration the positions of $N$ constituent objects, with $N$ itself
    part of the specification (the \emph{particle content}); $\Pi$ is a choice
    of exchange-symmetrization data on any repeated factors of $\Sigma^N$
    corresponding to identical constituents (the \emph{statistics}); and
    $\mathcal{A}$ is a distinguished algebra of ``local''/operationally
    accessible observables and couplings, determined by $\iota$ together with
    the locality structure of $\Sigma$, that fixes which perturbations and
    environmental interactions count as physically generic for the system. A
    \emph{physically interpreted Hamiltonian system} is a pair
    $\big((Q,\omega_{\mathrm{can}},H),\ \mathcal{D}\big)$.
\end{definition}

System A and System B are dynamically equivalent bare Hamiltonian systems
($N=1$ vs.\ $N=2$; $\iota_A$ vs.\ $\iota_B$; $\Pi$ trivial in A, potentially
non-trivial and empirically inert in the distant-particle regime of B;
$\mathcal{A}$ geometric-rotation-compatible in A, factorized/local in B) with
strictly inequivalent dressings. Dynamical equivalence of $(Q,H)$ places no
constraint whatsoever on $\mathcal{D}$, and conversely no amount of scrutiny of
$(Q,H)$ alone can recover $\mathcal{D}$: it is external data, supplied by
whoever is applying the formalism to a physical situation, and it is precisely
this data that answers questions like ``how many particles are there,'' ``does
rotating the coordinates correspond to rotating anything in the lab,'' and
``what does this system look like once coupled to a thermometer.''

This is, in effect, an elementary illustration of a point familiar from
elsewhere in the foundations of physics: mathematical structure that is
invariant under all automorphisms of a formalism can fail to fix physical
content, and conversely, seemingly different mathematical presentations can
hide the same physical content, or vice versa. The general-relativistic hole
argument is the best-known instance of the latter
phenomenon (a bare manifold-plus-metric under-determines ``which point is
which'' without further, non-dynamical identification); our example is closer
to the former --- here two \emph{physically distinct} situations happen to
share \emph{all} of their bare dynamical content, and only the dressing tells
them apart. In both cases the lesson is the same: read off only what the
invariant mathematical structure actually supports, and be explicit about what
further interpretive data is being assumed.

\subsection{Further Illustrations and Connections}

\paragraph{Normal modes.} The same moral appears, in a sense running the other
way, in the theory of coupled oscillators. A collection of masses connected by
springs, in general spatially local and physically coupled, has a Hamiltonian
that, after diagonalization in terms of normal-mode coordinates, takes exactly
the form of a set of \emph{decoupled} one-dimensional oscillators, $H=\sum_k
\big(P_k^2/2+\frac12\Omega_k^2 Q_k^2\big)$. If one only looked at this final,
diagonal $(Q,H)$, one might be tempted to read it as describing independent,
uncoupled particles. But the normal-mode coordinates $Q_k$ are collective
variables, each typically a linear combination of every original particle's
displacement; a perturbation local to one physical mass is, in normal-mode
space, generically \emph{non-local}, spreading across many or all of the $Q_k$.
This is the mirror image of subsection~\ref{loc}:
there, an abstractly ``local-looking''
coordinate split ($q_1,q_2$) failed to be the physically local one for System
A; here, an abstractly decoupled-looking Hamiltonian hides a physically
coupled, spatially local system. Both cases show that reading physical locality
or particle-independence directly off the form of $H$ in a given coordinate
system is unreliable without independent knowledge of the embedding.

\paragraph{Two-level systems.} A structurally similar warning applies to
quantum two-level systems. A spin-$\tfrac12$ degree of freedom and the two
lowest energy levels of an anharmonic trap are both described, abstractly, by
the Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^2$ and can be given identical effective
Hamiltonians; yet only in the spin case does the $SU(2)$ that acts on this
$\mathbb{C}^2$ correspond to actual rotations of physical space, with the
attendant consequences (e.g.\ magnetic dipole coupling, Wigner--Eckart
selection rules) that this geometric meaning brings. The bare qubit Hamiltonian
does not know which case it is in; the physical dressing does.

\paragraph{Configuration-space realism.} Our example also bears on a debate in
the foundations of quantum mechanics over how literally to take configuration
space --- especially the $3N$-dimensional configuration space of an
$N$-particle wavefunction --- as the fundamental arena in which physics takes
place. Whatever the merits of that view,
the present example shows that configuration space by itself does not even fix
\emph{how many particles} are being described, let alone where they are: the
same $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the configuration space of a one-particle and of a
two-particle system, and only the particle-content and embedding data --- the
dressing $\mathcal{D}$ --- decides which. Any
view that takes configuration space to be physically fundamental has to build
this data into the fundamental posit; it cannot be recovered from the space
itself.

\paragraph{A closing remark.} Because configuration space is used so widely ---
including, in some approaches to relativistic dynamics, as a stand-in for
spacetime itself, with an event or particle's spacetime location treated
formally as a configuration-space coordinate evolving in an external parameter
--- it is worth keeping the moral of this note in view whenever that move is
made: an isotropic-looking or symmetric-looking Hamiltonian on such a space
does not, by itself, certify that the associated symmetry is geometrically
real, or that a particular coordinate split corresponds to a physically
meaningful partition of the system, any more than it does for the two
oscillators of this note. That certification is always an additional physical
commitment.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The exact coincidence between the two-dimensional isotropic oscillator and two
distant, independent one-dimensional oscillators is real, and it survives every
construction --- classical, symplectic, or quantum --- that can be built from
the bare pair $(Q,H)$ alone. It is not, however, a paradox, and it does not
show that configuration space is physically meaningless. What it shows is that
$(Q,H)$ under-determines the physical system: the same bare Hamiltonian data
admits inequivalent \emph{physical dressings} --- inequivalent choices of how
many constituents there are, how the configuration coordinates are embedded in
physical space, whether a mathematical symmetry of $H$ corresponds to an actual
spatial symmetry, and which couplings to a wider environment count as
physically natural. Configuration space, on this view, is best treated as a
coordinatization adapted to, and only meaningful relative to, a prior
specification of what the system is --- a representation of the space of
instantaneous states, not a self-standing arena whose points carry physical
meaning independently of that specification. Making this dressing explicit,
rather than leaving it implicit, is cheap in the cases treated here; the value
of the exercise is in showing precisely what is being assumed whenever it is
left out.
