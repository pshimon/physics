\section{Microscopic Maxwell Equations}
\label{sec:ed-micro-maxwell-eqs}

Omitting the long history of misconceptions and discoveries, 
one may say that, in addition to mass, particles may possess 
electric charge. Like mass, it is a scalar quantity, but unlike mass, 
charge may be positive, negative, or zero.
To describe the interaction of charged particles, 
it is necessary to introduce a new entity --- the electromagnetic field $ (\bm E(\bm r,t),\bm B(\bm r,t)) $.

The motion of  particle with charge $ e $ is described by Lorentz force
\begin{equation}
   \label {lorentz-force}
   \frac{d \bm p}{d t}=e \bm E(\bm r,t) + \frac{e}{c}\bm v \times \bm B(\bm r,t)
\end{equation}
Dynamics of electromagnetic field is described   Maxwell’s equations:
\begin{align}
\nabla \times \bm{E} + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \bm{B}}{\partial t} &= 0, \label{me-faraday} \\
\nabla \cdot \bm{B} &= 0, \label{me-monopole} \\
\nabla \cdot \bm{E} &= 4\pi \rho, \label{me-gauss} \\
\nabla \times \bm{B} - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \bm{E}}{\partial t} &= \frac{4\pi}{c}\bm{j}. \label{me-ampere}
\end{align}
Here 
\begin{equation}
   \label {dens1}
    \rho(\bm r,t)=\Sigma_a e_a \delta (\bm r-\bm r_a)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
   \label {curr1}
    \bm j(\bm r,t)=\Sigma_a e_a \bm v_a \delta (\bm r-\bm r_a)
\end{equation}
It is important to know that $ \delta $-function is actually not a function, but distribution
--- linear functional on the space of test functions. It is very useful in linear problems
but makes a lot of troubles in non-linear ones.

Taking the divergence of \eqref{me-ampere} and using \eqref{me-gauss} 
yields the continuity equation:
\begin{equation}
    \label{cont}
\partial_t \rho + \nabla \cdot \bm{j} = 0,
\end{equation}
demonstrating that charge conservation is not an independent 
postulate but a structural consequence of Maxwell’s equations.
In particular \eqref{dens1} and \eqref{curr1} satisfy \eqref{cont}.

Maxwell equations (\eqref{me-faraday},\eqref{me-monopole},
\eqref{me-gauss},\eqref{me-ampere}) seem overdetermined:
eight scalar equations for six unknown field components. Actually they are not. 
Only the two curl equations (\eqref{me-faraday} and  \eqref{me-ampere}) contain time
hence are dynamic equations. The divergence equations (\eqref{me-gauss} and  
\eqref{me-gauss}) are conditions on the \emph{data}, not on the dynamics.

\section{Maxwell Equations in Media}
\label{sec:ed-maxwell-eqs}

In media we can somehow divide charges and currents into two groups: 
bound ones which belong to media and free ones.
Bound charges can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
   \label {bound-charge}
   \rho_b=-\nabla \bm P
\end{equation}
where $ \bm P $  (polarization) is the dipole moment of unit volume
\begin{equation}
   \label {bound-current}
   \bm j_b =\frac{\partial \bm P}{\partial t}+ c \nabla \times \bm M
\end{equation}
where $ \bm M$ (magnetization) is the magnetic dipole moment of unit volume.

Thus macroscopic (averaged) charge and current densities become
\begin{equation}
   \label {tot-cur-ch}
   \bm j=\bm j_f+ \bm j_b \qquad \rho=\rho_f+\rho_b
\end{equation}
and macroscopic Maxwell equations read:
\begin{align}
    \nabla \cdot \bm{E} &= 4\pi (\rho_f -\nabla \bm P), \label{me-g1} \\
    \nabla \times \bm{B} - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \bm{E}}{\partial t} &= 
    \frac{4\pi}{c}(\bm j_f+\frac{\partial \bm P}{\partial t}+ c \nabla \times \bm M). \label{me-a1}
\end{align}
or:
\begin{align}
    \nabla \cdot \bm D &= 4\pi \rho_f , \label{me-g2} \\
    \nabla \times \bm H - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \bm D}{\partial t} &= \frac{4\pi}{c}\bm j_f . \label{me-a2}
\end{align}
Here $ \bm D = \bm E+4 \pi \bm P $ and $ \bm H = \bm E-4 \pi \bm M $.
In equations \eqref{me-g2} and \eqref{me-a2} $ \rho $ and $ \bm j $ are supposed to be smooth
functions as a result of averaging of \eqref{dens1} and \eqref{curr1}.
Homogeneous equations \eqref{me-faraday} and \eqref{me-monopole} remain the same.  

Some information about media should be added to make Maxwell
equations solvable. Usually it is done by introducing constitutive
relations and generalized Ohm's law which define somehow 
$ \bm H $ , $ \bm D $ and $ \bm j $ in terms of 
$ \bm E $ and $ \bm B $. A lot of interesting physics
is contained in specific form of these relations.

\section{Gauge Transformations}
\label{sec:ed-gauge-transformations}

It is convenient to introduce scalar $\phi$ and vector $\bm A$ such that
\begin{equation}
   \label {potA}
   \bm B = \nabla \times \bm A \quad \bm E = -\frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \bm A}{\partial t} - \nabla \phi
\end{equation}
so the equations \eqref{me-monopole} and \eqref{me-faraday} become identities, remaining equations will
take the form
\begin{equation}
   \label {me-pot}
   \begin{split}
       \Box \bm A + \nabla ( \nabla \cdot \bm A+ \frac{1}{c} \dot \phi) &= \frac{4 \pi}{c} \bm j \\
       \Box \phi -\frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}( \nabla \cdot \bm A+ \frac{1}{c} \dot \phi) & = 4 \pi \rho
   \end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $ \Box $ is d'alambert operator defined as \[  \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}-\nabla^2 \]

Equations \eqref{potA} do not define potentials uniquely, gauge transformation: 
\begin{equation}
   \label {gauge-transformation}
   \begin{split}
       \bm A &\rightarrow \bm A+ \nabla \chi \\
       \phi & \rightarrow \phi - \dot \chi /c
   \end{split}
\end{equation}
 where $ \chi  $ is arbitrary leaves fields $ \bm B $
and $ \bm E $ unchanged. So we can prescribe additional
condition (gauge) on potentials.

If we prescribe the condition :
\begin{equation}
   \label {lg}
    \nabla \cdot \bm A+ \frac{1}{c} \dot \phi=0
\end{equation}
which is evidently compatible with \eqref{cont} equations \eqref{me-pot} separate and read:
\begin{equation}
   \label {me-pot-lg}
   \begin{split}
       \Box \bm A &= -\frac{4 \pi}{c} \bm j \\
       \Box \phi  & = -4 \pi \rho
   \end{split}
\end{equation}


The Lorenz gauge \eqref{lg} is the most popular one, it
allows us to uncouple equations \eqref{me-pot-lg}   so that the scalar and vector
potentials are described by symmetrical uncoupled wave equations,
which is a characteristic of this gauge. 
%It is the only covariant gauge.
However even Lorenz condition leaves some arbitrariness: $ \chi \rightarrow \chi + \psi $ 
 in \eqref{gauge-transformation} changes nothing if $ \Box \psi=0 $.

A less popular gauge in textbooks is the Coulomb gauge.
\begin{equation}
   \label {cg}
   \nabla \cdot \bm A=0
\end{equation}
In this gauge the scalar potential satisfies an instantaneous
Poisson equation, which is a peculiar characteristic of this
gauge.
A number of other gauges was studied in \cite{Heras2007}:
Kirchhoff gauge:
\begin{equation}
   \label {kg}
    \nabla \cdot \bm A- \frac{1}{c} \dot \phi=0
\end{equation}
which can be seen as Lorenz gauge in Euclidean 4-space.
Velocity gauge:
\begin{equation}
   \label {vg}
    \nabla \cdot \bm A- \frac{1}{v} \dot \phi=0
\end{equation}
The velocity gauge is one in which the scalar
potential propagates with an arbitrary speed. This gauge is
not well known despite the fact that it was proposed several
years ago. The v-gauge is really a family of gauges that
contains the Lorenz and Coulomb gauges as particular cases
and also includes the Kirchhoff gauge.

Temporal gauge:
\begin{equation}
   \label {tg}
   \phi=0
\end{equation}
 The temporal gauge is one in which 
the scalar potential is identically zero, which means that 
the electric and magnetic fields are defined 
only by the vector potential.

It is shown in \cite{Heras2007} that  all these  potentials yield the 
same  retarded
electric and magnetic fields. 

\section {Covariant Form of Maxwell Equations}
\label{sec:ed-me-cov}

Introducing (formally) fourth coordinate $ x^0=c t $ we can define
 Minkowski space as  the manifold $M \cong \mathbb{R}^4$ 
equipped with the  metric
\begin{equation}
   \label {minkg}
    g_{i j} = g^{i j}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1).
\end{equation} 
The meaning of it will be discussed later, meanwhile
it is used only to simplify notations. So
\begin{align}
    x^i&=(ct,\bm x) \label{x4} \\
    A^i&=(\phi,\bm A) \label{a4}\\
    j^i&=(c \rho, \bm j) \label{j4}\\
    \partial_i&= \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i} \label{d4}
\end{align}
So $ \Box=g^{i k} \partial_i \partial_k $ 
Lorenz gauge \eqref{lg} reads:
\begin{equation}
   \label {lgc}
   \partial_i A^i=0
\end{equation} 
 continuity equation \eqref{cont} becomes
\begin{equation}
   \label {contc}
   \partial_i j^i=0
\end{equation} 
and Maxwell equations \eqref{me-pot} take the form:
\begin{equation}
   \label {me-potc}
    \Box A^i= \frac{4 \pi}{c} j^i
\end{equation}
We can easily get wave equations for $ \bm E $ and $\bm B$.
Wave equations are a \emph{consequence} of Maxwell's equations, not an
equivalent reformulation. Differentiating raises the order, and the extra
solutions are spurious. Two  wave equations know nothing
about each other; it is the first-order system that couples $\bm E$ to
$\bm B$. Whenever we use the wave equation we must remember to impose
the first-order equations on the data.

Two remarks will matter later.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Applying $\partial_j$ to~\eqref{me-potc} and using
\eqref{contc} gives $\Box(\partial_j A^j)=0$. The Lorenz
condition is therefore itself a \emph{homogeneous wave equation}: it holds
for all times if \eqref{contc} and its time derivative vanish on one
initial surface. It is a condition on the data.
\item The Lorenz condition is preserved by a further gauge transformation
if and only if $\Box\chi=0$. The residual gauge freedom is itself a
homogeneous solution of the wave equation.
\end{enumerate}
So even the ambiguity in the description of the field lives in the space of
source-free solutions. This is the first hint that the homogeneous equation
is not an afterthought.

The fields $ (\bm E,\bm B) $ in covariant formalism
become parts of antisymmetric tensor of second rank:
\begin{equation}
   \label {ebc}
    F_{i k}=\partial_i A_k-\partial_k A_i
\end{equation}
where $ A_k=g_{k i} A^i=(\phi,-\bm A) $ so equations \eqref{me-gauss}
and \eqref{me-ampere} take the form
\begin{equation}
   \label {mec}
    \partial_k F^{i k}=-\frac{4 \pi}{c} j^k
\end{equation}

One can easily see that Maxwell equations save their form
under linear transformations preserving metric \eqref{minkg}.
However Galilean transformations change them, in particular
the velocity of light does not depend on inertial frame of
reference, which contradicts the rule of adding velocities.


It is often stated that the fields $ (\bm E,\bm B) $ or
$ F_{i k} $ are the physical variables and potentials
$A^i$ have no physical meaning. This statement based
on fact that potentials $ A^i $ are not gauge invariant.
However with argument is not sound, following this logic
we must declare canonical momenta as not physical since
it  is also not gauge invariant (see Section~\ref{sec:cm-sym}).
In both cases there is specific gauge which gives direct physical
meaning, it is Lorenz gauge in case of potentials and
zero total derivative in Lagrangian in case of momenta.


\section{Physics of Electromagnetic Field}
\label{sec:ed-phys-em}

Any solution of wave equation may be represented as linear combination of specific
solution of inhomogeneous equation and general solution of homogeneous one:
\begin{equation}
   \label {we-sol}
    A_i(x)=\int G_{ik}(x,y)j^k(y) d^4 y +A_i^{free} (x)
\end{equation}
where $  G_{ik}(x,y)$ is a Green's function.
On the other hand potential determines the motion of charges by Lorentz force \eqref{lorentz-force}
and hence currents. Generally the self-consistent problem can not be solved,
it can not be even correctly posed.

However we can split the problem, for now we shall consider
currents $ j^k(x) $ as fixed.
The particular part is generated by the sources. The homogeneous part is
generated by nothing in the region under consideration.

An analogy from electrostatics is instructive. A solution of
$\nabla^2\phi=0$ in a region $\Omega$ vanishes identically if $\phi=0$ on
$\partial\Omega$ (maximum principle); the uniform field inside a capacitor
exists only because the plates carry non-zero boundary values. A free
solution is the shadow of its boundary data. The wave equation obeys the
same logic, with one difference: the ``boundary'' now includes the initial
surface, and since the equation is second order in time the data on it
consist of two functions (the field and its time derivative; for Maxwell's
equations, $\bm E$ and $\bm B$).

With the convention $\Box G=\delta(t)\,\delta^3(\mathbf x)$ there are two
natural Green functions,
\begin{equation}
G_{\rm ret}(\mathbf x,t)=\frac{\delta(t-r/c)}{4\pi r},\qquad
G_{\rm adv}(\mathbf x,t)=\frac{\delta(t+r/c)}{4\pi r},
\qquad r=|\mathbf x| .
\label{ed-green}
\end{equation}
Each gives a particular solution, $A^\mu_{\rm ret/adv}=\mu_0\,G_{\rm ret/adv}*J^\mu$.
Their difference
\begin{equation}
G_{\rm ret}-G_{\rm adv}=\frac{\delta(t-r/c)-\delta(t+r/c)}{4\pi r}
\label{ed-pauli-jordan}
\end{equation}
satisfies $\Box(G_{\rm ret}-G_{\rm adv})=0$ everywhere, including at $r=0$,
where it is regular as a distribution (it behaves as $-\delta'(t)/2\pi c$).
Hence $A_{\rm ret}-A_{\rm adv}$ is a perfectly good homogeneous solution,
generated by the sources but source-free as a field. 

In a region free of charges one may choose $\phi=0$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$,
and the general homogeneous solution is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathbf A_{\rm h}(\mathbf x,t)=\int\!\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\sum_{\lambda=1,2}
\Big[a_\lambda(\mathbf k)\,&\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\lambda(\mathbf k)\,
e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega_k t)}+\text{c.c.}\Big],\\
&\omega_k=c|\mathbf k|,\qquad
\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\lambda=0 .
\end{split}
\label{ed-modes}
\end{equation}
There are two polarizations per wave vector. This is the content of the
word ``photon'' at the classical level: the amplitudes $a_\lambda(\mathbf k)$
are free data, unconstrained by any source.

A non-trivial
homogeneous solution is one whose Cauchy data $(\mathbf E,\mathbf B)$ do
not vanish somewhere on every spacelike slice; equivalently, in a
scattering situation, one that carries incoming radiation from past
infinity, or outgoing radiation to future infinity. In particular, ``no
incoming radiation'' is a \emph{boundary condition}, and it is the one
that turns the retarded solution into the solution of a physical problem.

\textbf{Caveat on what counts as data.}  Data of a different
kind, for example the field alone on two different time slices, do not
suffice; we will see below a non-trivial
solution that vanishes on the \emph{entire} boundary of a space-time box.
This distinction turns out to be central.


\section{Lagrangian for Electromagnetic Field}
\label{sec:ed-lag-em}

Electromagnetic field is an example of continuous system, where
dynamic variables depend not only on $ t $ but also on $ \bm x $.
Such systems may be seen as infinite set of "usual" variables, so
that dependence on $ \bm x $ is a generalisation of index, numbering
"particles". Another (and I think more reasonable) approach is to
consider any component of the  field as as degree of freedom depending
on multidimensional "time".

Anyway Lagrange function is represented as space integral of 
Lagrange density and action becomes 4-dimensional integral.
The most important is the change in boundaries, indeed instead
two points in phase space, boundary becomes to 3-dimensional
surface $ \partial V $ of 4-dimensional volume $ V $.

Equations \eqref{mec} follow from action integral
\begin{equation}
   \label {em-ac-1}
    S_1=-\frac{1}{c^2} \int A_i j^i d^4 x-\frac{1}{16 \pi c} \int F_{ik} F^{ik} d^4 x
\end{equation}
and equations \eqref{me-potc} can be obtained from
action
Equations \eqref{mec} follow from action integral
\begin{equation}
   \label {em-ac-2}
    S_2=-\frac{1}{c^2} \int A_i j^i d^4 x-\frac{1}{8 \pi c} \int \partial_i A^k \partial^i A_k d^4 x 
\end{equation}
here potential $ A^i $ should be in Lorenz gauge \eqref{lgc}.
It is no longer implied by the equations of motion; it must be imposed on the
free solutions, where it is a homogeneous wave equation with its own
boundary data


Every textbook on electrodynamics contains the following two statements.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Maxwell's equations imply that the fields (and, in a suitable gauge,
the potentials) satisfy the wave equation. Without sources this equation
still has non-trivial solutions: light.
\item Maxwell's equations follow from a least-action principle. One varies
the potentials, integrates by parts, ``neglects the total divergence'',
and reads off the field equations.
\end{enumerate}

Both statements are correct. Placed side by side, however, they hide a
tension. A source-free solution of a linear hyperbolic equation can be
different from zero only if \emph{something} is different from zero on the
boundary of the region in which we solve it. The variational principle, on
the other hand, is derived by throwing the boundary away.

The thesis of this chapter is that these are \emph{one fact seen from two
sides}. The total divergences that we discard in the action are precisely
the place where the source-free solutions of the wave equation---what
quantum theory calls photons---are visible to the action at all. Discarding
them is harmless for obtaining the field equations. It is not harmless for
what the field equations \emph{cannot} tell us: which free radiation is
present, what the symplectic structure of the theory is, and in which
direction time runs.
derivatives; they are the evolution equations.

Charge conservation is not an additional assumption: it is a necessary
condition for the sourced equations to have any solution at all.

Let integral in \eqref{em-ac-1} is taken over 
a space-time region $V$. Since the current is considered to be fixed
variation $ \delta S_1 $ is
\[
    \delta S_1=-\frac{1}{c}\int_V d^4x \left\{ \frac{1}{c} j^i \delta A_i
    -\frac{1}{4 \pi} F^{ik} \pd{\delta A_i}{x^k}\right\}
\]
integration by parts gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{em-ac-1-var}
\delta S_1=-\frac{1}{c}\int_V d^4x \left\{ \frac{1}{c} j^i 
 +\frac{1}{4 \pi}  \pd{F^{ik}}{x^k} \right\}\delta A_i
    -\frac{1}{4 \pi c} \oint d \Sigma_k F^{ik}\delta A_i
\end{equation}
The first term yields the field equations \eqref{mec}.
The second is the total divergence we ``neglect''. For
$V=[t_1,t_2]\times\Omega$ it splits into a contribution from the two time
slices and one from the spatial boundary.

The usual argument sets $\delta A_i=0$ on $\partial V$ and the surface
term disappears. Three observations on this step.
\begin{description}
    \item [What vanishes is the variation, not the field.] The
background field $A$ may be non-zero on $\partial V$. The term
$\mathbf E\cdot\delta\mathbf A$ vanishes because $\delta\mathbf A=0$, not
because $\mathbf E=0$. The photon content, if any, sits in $\mathbf E$ and
is invisible to this step.
\item [Fixing $A$ at both ends is a two-point problem.] Physical
problems are initial-value problems: we know the past and want the future.
Holding $A$ fixed at $t_1$ \emph{and} $t_2$ asks for something we do not
know in advance, and the two-point problem for a wave equation need not
have a solution, nor a unique one. In
particle mechanics the same issue is hidden by the fact that Hamilton's
principle is used only to \emph{derive} the equations of motion.
\item [Spatial infinity is subtler than it looks.] For sources that
were switched on at a finite time the retarded field vanishes at fixed $t$
as $r\to\infty$ (the radiation has not arrived yet), so the spatial term
vanishes on every finite time slab. But radiation falls off only as $1/r$
while the area of a sphere grows as $r^2$, so surface integrals of
quadratic expressions such as $\delta\mathbf A\times\mathbf B$ over a
sphere in the radiation zone are $O(1)$, not zero. The energy that leaves
the system crosses precisely such spheres.
\end{description}

In the terminology of this book the situation is a clean example of two
worlds that should not be mixed. Causality, ``what has actually been
radiated'' and ``what we see in a detector'' belong to the real world. The
variational principle, with its fixed endpoints and discarded divergences,
is a construction of the ideal world: time-symmetric, and blind to
boundary data by design. If one asks the ideal-world construction to
reproduce a real-world fact (the retarded, causal behavior of radiation, or
the number of photons in a beam), it cannot. The information was removed in
the step ``neglect the divergence'', and has to be put back in by hand as a
boundary condition. The pattern is the same as in the other examples of this book: a
rigorous derivation inside the ideal world is asked a question that belongs
to the real world.

There are other places where discarded divergences matter.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stress tensor.} The canonical energy--momentum tensor of the
Maxwell field is not symmetric, and the Belinfante symmetric tensor differs
from it by a divergence. The total four-momentum agrees if the surface term
vanishes. For radiation, which falls only as $1/r$, the corresponding
surface integrals for \emph{angular momentum} do not vanish automatically,
and the split into ``spin'' and ``orbital'' parts of the angular
momentum of light is sensitive to which surface terms are discarded.
\item \textbf{The $\theta$-term.} The Lagrangian $\theta\,F_{ik}\tilde F^{ik}$ is a pure divergence,
\begin{equation*}
F_{ik}\tilde F^{ik}=2\,\partial_i\big(\varepsilon^{ik\rho\sigma}A_k\partial_\rho A_\sigma\big),
\end{equation*}
proportional to $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B$ (the sign depends on the convention
for $\varepsilon^{0123}$). It changes no local field equation. It matters,
however, for non-trivial boundary data or topology (Chern--Simons terms,
instantons), where the divergence cannot be integrated away.
\item \textbf{Gravity.} The Einstein--Hilbert action is second order in
derivatives of the metric; the boundary term that makes the variational
principle well posed with fixed boundary metric (Gibbons--Hawking--York) is
a divergence that cannot be dropped.
\end{itemize}
